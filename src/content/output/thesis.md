Recently, I finished my PhD thesis in Theoretical High Energy Physics (string theory), titled 'The fermionic form of giant gravitons'.
Below is my attempt to explain it in ten sentences.[^a]

1. At its deepest, the world as we know it is described by two physical theories with irreconcilable differences: *quantum field theory* (QFT) and *general relativity* (GR).
2. In order to obtain the full picture of nature, we need to understand the *quantum states* of gravity, which are characterised by particles called *gravitons*—this requires a new theory that coherently integrates both QFT and GR.
3. After decades of trying, the only lead we have is *string theory*: a quantum theory that says that, what we perceived as particles and forces of nature, are just approximations from 'far away'—from up close, they reveal themselves as different forms of the same tiny rubber band-like *string*.
4. A crucial insight was that string theory is not just a theory of strings, but that it contains larger and heavier structures called *branes*—nobody introduced branes into string theory by hand but mathematical consistency requires their existence.
5. From far away, the quantum states of gravity are then not just gravitons (due to individual strings), but also *giant gravitons* (due to branes)—interpreted as gravitons that have 'blown' up into large, balloon-like objects.
6. The quantum states of these larger objects are still poorly understood, which has led people to study simpler toy models and subsequently uncover a phenomenon called *holography*.
7. Holographic universes have the special property that they can be fully described by an ordinary quantum field theory living on their 'boundary' (think of the ingredients list on a can of soup describing its contents).
8. Since quantum field theories are better understood than string theory, holography opened up a new avenue into the study of string theory's large objects, which I used in my thesis.
9. Quantum states generally come in two kinds: *bosons* like the graviton or photon (usually associated with forces) and *fermions* like the electron or proton (usually associated with matter).
10. While gravitons—describing the gravitational force—are bosons, I found in my thesis that the quantum states of their giant cousins surprisingly admit a purely fermionic description: *The fermionic form of giant gravitons*.
